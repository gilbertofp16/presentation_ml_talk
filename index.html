[1:50 · ~1m10s] GenAI changed the economics of the prototype. One afternoon, one API call, and you have something that impresses a room.

The trap is that the demo feels 80% done. In reality it's the visible 20%. The rest is technical (edge cases, latency, cost, privacy, monitoring), but it's also people and process.

Production needs a proper development lifecycle. It needs the right engineers, and the right leader to unblock them and push the team forward. A demo needs none of that. That's why it's so easy, and why it's so misleading.

And the one teams skip most: measuring the right impact. If all we do is put tech around the model, we have a better demo, not a product. Production needs a baseline, the right metric, and a clear link to a business goal. That's section two.

[3:25 · ~1m10s] First, the numbers. MIT's 2025 study found that about 95% of enterprise GenAI pilots show no measurable P&L impact. And RAND's 2024 study found the number one root cause of AI project failure is misunderstanding the problem, so the model optimises the wrong metric. Not the model. The problem definition.

Researchers love the bottom layer: accuracy, F1, faithfulness. Necessary, but nobody signs a budget for F1.

Start at the top. Which decision or process changes? What does it cost today? That's your baseline. Then: how much has to move, and how much speed, revenue or cost are we actually winning?

And the question teams forget: which company OKR does this serve? If you can't connect it to an objective leadership already cares about, it loses every prioritisation fight.

[4:35 · ~1m10s] A few years ago at SEEK. To be fair to them: SEEK has always been strong in AI and is a very mature AI organisation today. This was before GenAI. We used data science, transformers and deep learning for specific problems, so projects took much longer than they do now.

There were plenty of ideas to improve prototypes, and one area was matching candidates' CVs to jobs. A typical project: more than six months, a team of four.

And sometimes, after six months, it never got released. Not because we did a bad job. The models were good. When the project started, we never set a metric that reflected what we actually wanted to achieve once a candidate was matched.

That hurts more than the roadmap. When you work six months and nothing ships, careers stall too. People need releases to grow.

[5:45 · ~1m] Looking back, these are the questions we should have answered on day one.

  • How much time does a recruiter save when we match a CV to a job? That's the value, in a unit the business understands.
  • Which industries are most keen to use it? That tells you where to launch.
  • And the one nobody asks: which industries don't benefit at all? Some get so many candidates that better matching adds nothing. Knowing that early saves months.

And the promises. People would say "this will increase applications by 10%", or 5%. Do we really get 10%? How do we know? Can we commit to that number this quarter, and does it represent what the team will actually deliver? Nobody could answer that.

None of these is a model question. All of them decide whether the model ever ships.

[6:45 · ~1m15s] After SEEK I went to Mr Yum, and this time I knew what mistake not to make.

We wanted a recommendation system that suggests items before checkout. The premise: better usability, and people buy more. But before building any model, my team tested whether that theory was true.

How? We compared restaurants that already had really good recommendations set up manually with restaurants that didn't. The manual version is the prototype. Not perfectly accurate, but if humans doing it by hand moves the number, an AI replicating the human can too.

The first finding: 40% of menu items had no recommendation at all. Restaurants didn't want to set them up, didn't keep their online menus fresh, and doing it manually takes too much time.

[8:00 · ~1m15s] Then we compared spend. Restaurants with full recommendations showed roughly 20 to 30% higher cart spend than restaurants with none. Restaurants with about half their items covered didn't even reach 5%.

So we saw three kinds of value. Usability: a nicer way to discover items. Automation: the restaurant gets recommendations without doing the work. But the biggest one was the lost opportunity: 40% of items with no recommendation means money left on the table on every order.

2 · Prove the impact · Story 2: doing it right

One primary metric, tied to the OKR

Primary metric

Cross-sell spend per order

Secondary

Time saved for restaurants · automation

Why only one? Marry too many metrics and nothing is objective. One metric explains how you support the OKR.

  1. 3 monthsBuild
  2. Beta · 15 restaurants+10% cross-sell
  3. Full rollout · 2 months later+23–24% cross-sell
  4. Company OKRMore spend per shopping cart

[9:15 · ~1m15s] So the metric chose itself: increase cross-sell spend per order. That was our primary success metric. Secondary ones existed (restaurants spending less time, more automation), but we committed to one.

Why not marry a lot of metrics? Because then nothing is objective and it's hard to track. You want one main metric that explains how you support the OKR and the company goal.

Results: about three months to build, then a beta with 15 restaurants. We saw a 10% increase and released to everyone. Two months later, cross-sell was up around 23 to 24%.

And the company OKR was to increase what people spend in the shopping cart. So we could say, with evidence, that we moved it by around 24%. That's the difference from SEEK: same kind of team, but we defined the finish line first.

3 · Build for production · My golden rule

An AI system is only as good as the software engineering around it.

We overestimate the model and underestimate the software.

[10:30 · ~40s] Pause on this one. It's the core of the talk.

Every AI idea has to be held to the same engineering bar as any other software: tests, reviews, monitoring, ownership. Data science and ML are often treated as special, so they skip that bar, and that's exactly why they break in production.

[11:10 · ~50s] Many of you will know this figure from the Sculley paper. The model code is the small box in the middle. Everything else is configuration, data pipelines, serving and monitoring.

And the numbers back it up. Gartner's 2024 survey: only 48% of AI projects make it into production, and it takes about 8 months to get from prototype to production. RAND estimates more than 80% of AI projects fail, twice the rate of normal IT projects, and lack of infrastructure to deploy models is one of the root causes.

GenAI made that box even smaller. The model is now an API call. So almost all of the work, and all of the risk, sits in the surrounding software. If your team is all model and no software, you'll stay in demo land.

[12:00 · ~1m30s] The lifecycle has changed a lot since ChatGPT came out.

In the data science era: we defined the metrics to decide whether the project was worth it, then built the data pipelines and orchestration, experimented, trained a model, registered it, moved to staging, then production, then monitoring. Each step took a long time. Months before anyone saw real results. That's the SEEK story.

What changed is speed. Faster demos, faster code. So we get to staging much earlier and see, with real data, whether we're on track for the business metric.

Let's be realistic though: it's not weeks. We still do staging, monitoring, everything. What used to take four months or more now takes about one month. That's what I've lived over the last two years.

[13:30 · ~45s] The engineer's role has already changed. Writing features, boilerplate, tests and docs: agents do most of that now.

Today the job is owning the decisions: the architecture, the flows and boundaries, keeping things simple instead of over-engineered, and being accountable for what ships. We're not correcting code anymore. We're correcting decisions.

And it will keep shifting: less typing, more judgement.

But here's the thing. Models change every year. In my 12 years in AI, one thing has never changed: the human in the loop. That's what gives AI its direction. So let me show you how we keep it there.

[14:15 · ~2m] This is our agentic engineering framework. Two lanes: what the engineer does and what the agent does. And under each step, why we do it, backed by research.

  • 1. Set the direction (engineer). A task, a Jira ticket or a bug fix. Decisions get written down, not left in a chat, because in Anthropic's Project Vend, Claude running a small shop gave stock away, reversed decisions and invented details.
  • 2. Read and plan (agent). It reads the code, rules and ADRs, then writes a phased plan with a diagram. Anthropic found agents try to do too much at once and leave a mess, so: one task at a time, progress written down.
  • 3. Approve the plan (engineer). No code before "go". Laban et al. found leading models are about 39% worse in long multi-turn work, and once they take a wrong turn they don't recover. The plan is where a mistake is cheapest.
  • 4. Build in stages (agent). Proof at every stage. In Vending-Bench, every model had runs that went off the rails, and Anthropic found agents declare success too early and skip real testing.
  • 5. Review and merge (engineer). Peer review plus automated checks. Anthropic advises human approval for anything with real consequences.

So not every step is agentic, by design. And this isn't slowing us down for no reason: it's what Anthropic itself recommends. Explore, plan, code, commit.

Q&A backup: in Anthropic's agentic misalignment study, Claude Opus 4 took the harmful action 55.1% of the time when it believed the scenario was real, vs 6.5% when it believed it was a test (500 samples). Same model, very different behaviour when stakes feel real: the reason for human approval on irreversible actions.

Plan before code: the development plan

  • Context firstThe agent reads code, docs and tests before planning.
  • Extend, don't rewriteReuse existing patterns. Architecture is preserved by default.
  • Unknowns made explicitNo silent guessing to fill the gaps.
  • Checkable phases & QA gatesProgress you can verify, not "trust me".
  • Stop pointA human approves before any code is written.
  • Use the skillgithub.com/gilbertofp16/gen_ai_repo

[16:15 · ~1m] I packaged this as a reusable agent skill, the development planner. When an agent gets a non-trivial task, it has to produce this file first.

  • It starts with context gathering: read the code, docs and tests. No planning in a vacuum.
  • It extends existing patterns instead of rewriting them. That's how you keep architecture intact when agents write a lot of code.
  • Unknowns and assumptions are written down, so the agent doesn't quietly invent requirements.
  • Phases are checklists with a QA gate, so verification is objective.
  • And it stops. A human reviews the plan before a single line is written. That's where accountability sits.

Reviewing a one-page plan is far cheaper than reviewing a 2,000-line diff built on the wrong assumption.

The skill is open on my GitHub. The link is on the slide if you want to use it.

[17:15 · ~1m10s] Before I approve a plan, I ask the agent to draw it. Usually Mermaid, sometimes draw.io.

In ten seconds I can see what's new, what changes, what stays the same, and in which phase each piece gets built. If the architecture is wrong, or it's over-engineered, I catch it here, before a single line of code.

It also helps the team: whoever reviews the PR has already seen the picture, so they know what to expect and review much faster.

[18:30 · ~35s] Once the plan is approved, this is how we run it to the end.

One: build one stage, the code and its unit tests, only for the next phase. Two: prove it. Unit tests pass, and we do manual testing when it's needed. That's a loop: build, prove, build, prove, until the task, the ticket or the bug fix is done.

Three: approve and ship. The PR carries the plan and the diagram, so the human review is much faster. And our normal CI/CD runs, exactly as before.

We're not changing how engineering is done. We're executing it faster and more automatically, so people can focus on reviewing and deciding instead of writing every line.

[19:00 · ~45s] Remember the goal: think twice. Here's what to do at each of those moments.

  • Before you accept a project: define the metric of success. One primary metric, tied to an OKR. That's the difference between my first story and my second.
  • Before you commit to a number: investigate first. Test the theory with what exists today. That's how we found the 40% before building anything.
